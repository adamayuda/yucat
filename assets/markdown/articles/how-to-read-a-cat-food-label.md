# How to Read a Cat Food Label Without Getting Fooled

*"Natural", "premium" and "with real chicken" are legally close to meaningless. Here is what the back of the pack actually tells you.*

![A curious cat looking over the edge of a box](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1514888286974-6c03e2ca1dba?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1200&q=80)
*Everything that matters is printed in the smallest type on the bag.*

---

## Start at the bottom: the adequacy statement

Before ingredients, before protein, find the one sentence that says the food is **complete and balanced**. In the US it cites AAFCO; in Europe, FEDIAF.

There are two ways a food earns it, and they are not equal:

| Wording | What it means |
|---|---|
| "…formulated to meet the nutrient levels established by…" | Checked on a spreadsheet |
| "…animal feeding tests… substantiate that it provides complete nutrition" | Actually fed to cats and measured |

Feeding-trial foods are the stronger claim. Also check the **life stage**: growth, adult maintenance, or *all life stages*. An "all life stages" food is formulated for kittens, which means it is energy-dense — fine for a growing cat, easy overfeeding for a neutered eight-year-old.

> If a package has no adequacy statement at all, it is a **treat or complementary food**, not a meal. That is legal and fine, as long as you know it.

---

## The ingredient list, and its favourite trick

Ingredients are listed by weight **before cooking**. That single detail creates most of the confusion.

### Fresh meat is mostly water

"Fresh chicken" is around 70% water. After extrusion, most of that weight is gone. A bag listing *chicken* first can still finish with less chicken protein than one listing **chicken meal** first, because meal is already rendered and dried — roughly 300% more protein by weight than fresh.

**Chicken meal is not a downgrade.** A named meal is a concentrated protein source.

### Named beats unnamed, always

- **Good:** chicken meal, lamb meal, salmon meal, turkey liver
- **Vague:** meat meal, poultry by-product meal, animal fat, meat and animal derivatives

The problem with unnamed sources is not that they are poison — it is that the supplier can change between batches. For a cat with a food allergy, that is the whole ball game.

### Ingredient splitting

Watch for one ingredient entered several times under different names:

> Chicken, **peas**, chicken meal, **pea protein**, **pea fibre**, **pea starch**…

Individually each sits below the chicken. Added together, peas may outweigh it. Legal, common, and worth spotting.

![Bread and wheat, a stand-in for cereal fillers](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1509440159596-0249088772ff?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1200&q=80)
*Grain is not automatically bad. Grain doing the job protein should be doing is.*

## The guaranteed analysis, and the maths everyone skips

The panel gives crude protein, fat, fibre and moisture. You cannot compare a wet food to a dry food using these numbers directly, because one is 78% water and the other is 8%.

Convert both to **dry matter basis**:

```
Dry matter % = (as-fed %) / (100 - moisture %) × 100
```

Worked example:

| | Wet food | Dry food |
|---|---|---|
| Protein as printed | 10% | 32% |
| Moisture | 78% | 8% |
| **Protein, dry matter** | **45.5%** | **34.8%** |

The wet food that *looked* like a third of the protein has considerably more. This is the single most useful calculation in pet nutrition, and almost no one does it at the shelf.

### Rough targets, dry matter basis

| Nutrient | Adult cat | Kitten |
|---|---|---|
| Protein | 40%+ | 45%+ |
| Fat | 18–30% | 20–35% |
| Carbohydrate | under 20% is preferable | as low as practical |

Carbohydrate is almost never printed. Estimate it:

```
Carbs ≈ 100 - protein - fat - fibre - ash - moisture
```

If ash is missing, assume about 6–8% for a typical complete food.

## Things that must be there

- **Taurine.** Cats cannot make enough themselves. Deficiency causes irreversible blindness and dilated cardiomyopathy. Every complete cat food lists it.
- **Vitamin A, preformed.** Cats cannot convert beta-carotene the way dogs and humans can.
- **Arachidonic acid**, from animal fat. This is one of several reasons a cat cannot be safely vegan.

---

## Claims worth ignoring

- **"Natural"** — loosely defined, permits plenty
- **"Human-grade"** — meaningful only if the *entire* product is made in a human food facility
- **"Grain-free"** — replaces grain with potato or pea, often at similar carbohydrate levels
- **"Holistic", "premium", "gourmet"** — no legal definition whatsoever
- **"With chicken"** — in many jurisdictions can mean as little as **4%**

The picture of a salmon fillet on the front is art direction. The ingredient list is the contract.

---

## A 60-second shelf routine

1. Find the adequacy statement, and check the life stage
2. Read the first five ingredients — are they named animal sources?
3. Scan for splitting: the same vegetable under three names
4. Convert protein to dry matter and compare like with like
5. Estimate carbs by subtraction
6. Confirm taurine is listed
7. Only now look at the price per kilo

---

## The short version

A cat is an obligate carnivore that evolved eating small animals: high protein, moderate fat, very little carbohydrate, and about 70% water. Judge a label by how close it gets to that, and let the marketing on the front of the bag argue with itself.

---

*Ask your vet before changing the diet of a cat with kidney disease, diabetes, urinary or gastrointestinal conditions. Those cases have targets of their own.*
